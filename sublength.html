Why sub-length matters

Every pixel accumulates two things during an exposure: signal from your target, and noise from several sources — sky background shot noise, the camera's read noise, and dark current shot noise. Stacking many subs averages out random noise, but read noise is paid once per sub, so short subs pay it more often per hour of total integration. The goal is a sub just long enough that sky-background shot noise dominates the noise budget, so read noise becomes negligible — beyond that point, longer subs buy almost nothing extra per hour, they just risk saturating bright cores and cost you more frames lost to satellites, wind, and guiding blips.

Sky-noise-limited exposure length

For a chosen tolerance — how much extra stacked noise you'll accept above the theoretical (zero read-noise) minimum — the optimal sub length has a closed form. If k is the accepted noise multiplier (e.g. k = 1.05 for 5% extra noise) and C = 1 / (k² − 1):

t_opt = C · R² / (P + D)

where R is read noise (e⁻), P is the sky-background electron rate (e⁻/px/s), and D is dark current (e⁻/px/s) — the two shot-noise sources are added together in the denominator, since both are just Poisson noise that grows with exposure time the same way. A 5% penalty gives C≈10, a 2% penalty gives C≈25 — the curve flattens fast, which is why the app defaults to a 5% target rather than chasing zero. (This matches the standard community formula for this method, e.g. as implemented in Deep Sky Detail's sub-length calculator.)

Estimating sky electron rate

Sky background is an extended source, so — unlike point-source targets — its per-pixel signal rate depends only on focal ratio and pixel size, not aperture directly (a faster system delivers more sky photons per pixel regardless of how big the objective is):

P = K · (pixel_µm)² / f_ratio² · QE · τ_filter · 10^(0.4·(SQM_ref − SQM))

Here τ_filter is the fraction of broadband sky-glow the filter passes — approximated from the filter's total bandpass width relative to a ~300nm broadband reference, so a 12nm dual-narrowband filter transmits roughly 4% of the sky-glow continuum a broadband filter would. K is a calibration constant tuned to typical field-reported background rates; it and SQM_ref are engineering approximations, not a laboratory-calibrated zero point.

This theoretical estimate is a starting point, not ground truth. The most accurate number comes from switching to Measured mode and reading the mean background ADU straight off an actual test sub taken with your gain and filter — the app converts that directly to e⁻/s using your camera's e⁻/ADU at that gain.

Saturation ceiling

Independent of noise theory, a sub can simply run too long and clip. The app caps recommended length so sky background alone never exceeds a configurable fraction of full well (default 70%), leaving headroom for the target's own signal:

t_sat = headroom · FullWell(e⁻) / P

This protects against sky background saturation only. It does not protect against target saturation.

Bright-star saturation cap

A star is a point source: it delivers far more photons per pixel per second than the diffuse sky ever does, so a sub can be nowhere near sky-saturated and still clip every bright star in the field — this is completely normal in narrowband imaging, where sky-noise-limited exposures often land in the 5–20 minute range while a bright star saturates in seconds. The app offers three ways to bound this, in increasing order of trust:

Flat cap (no data needed): a simple, editable practical ceiling (default 480s / 8min), independent of the noise and sky-saturation math. It's a guess, not a computed value — useful when you haven't looked anything up.

Theoretical (needs a star magnitude from Stellarium): frame the actual target at your rig's real FOV and rotation, read off the magnitude of the brightest star in shot, and enter it. The app computes a peak-pixel electron rate from first principles and derives the time until it saturates:

e_rate(peak) = N₀ · 10^(−0.4·mag) · A_aperture(cm²) · τ_filter · QE · frac(σ_px) t_star_sat = FullWell(e⁻) / e_rate(peak)

where N₀ is the photon flux from a magnitude-0 star integrated across the same ~300nm reference broadband used for τ_filter above (so the same filter that cuts sky-glow continuum cuts stellar continuum the same way — a real, if approximate, physical link), A_aperture is the telescope's clear aperture area, and frac(σ_px) is the fraction of the star's Gaussian PSF landing in the single peak pixel, properly integrated over the pixel (not just point-sampled) from your entered seeing/FWHM and the rig's pixel scale. Treat this as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a trustworthy number — it depends on an unverified absolute zero-point and an idealised PSF shape, and testing found it can disagree with real-world observation by a meaningful factor.

Calibrated (recommended, needs one real data point): instead of trusting a theoretical zero-point, give the app one measured reference — a star of known magnitude and the peak ADU it actually reached in a real sub of known length, at the gain currently selected. The app scales to your target star purely via the magnitude flux ratio:

e_rate(peak, target) = [(ADU_ref − bias) · e⁻/ADU / t_ref] · 10^(−0.4·(mag_target − mag_ref))

This sidesteps the zero-point and PSF-shape guesswork entirely — the only physics involved is the magnitude system's flux ratio, which is exact — so as long as the reference star was shot at the same gain and filter as your planned session, this is the number to trust.

Whichever cap is active, the final recommendation is min(t_opt, t_sat, t_star_cap), and the badge tells you which one is actually binding and which method produced it.

Guiding match

Guide RMS is a near-continuous correction residual, not something that grows with sub length once your guide loop is faster than a few seconds — so it doesn't set a hard time ceiling the way noise and saturation do. What it does set is whether your stars stay round at your pixel scale. The rule of thumb used here: guide RMS at or below ~30% of pixel scale gives essentially round stars; above ~100% of pixel scale, expect visible elongation.

Caveats

  • Gain→read-noise/full-well curves are seeded from published manufacturer figures and community measurements where manufacturer PDFs weren't directly accessible — edit them in the Gear Library if you have your own measured values.
  • The theoretical sky-rate model is a relative, order-of-magnitude estimate calibrated against typical reported values, not a photometric instrument. Measured mode (an actual test sub) is always more accurate for your specific sky, moon phase, and light-pollution conditions on the night in question.
  • Dark current is assumed near-zero for a cooled camera at typical setpoints unless you override it.
